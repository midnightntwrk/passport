Build notes for the NEXT production deploy. Rewrite this file before every deploy; `scripts/tag-release.mjs` refuses to tag without a "## Fixed" section.

## Fixed
- **A slow node left an abandoned connection open every time the sponsor gave up on it** (stagenet sponsor, 2026/09/07). When an attempt to open a connection takes too long the sponsor stops waiting and reports the failure — but the attempt itself carried on in the background, so a node that was merely slow rather than unreachable would finish opening a perfectly good connection a moment later that nothing was holding on to. Each one stayed open for the rest of the day, with its own reconnect machinery running behind it, and the drill that afternoon produced one. Any connection that arrives after the sponsor has stopped waiting for it is now closed, and a line is written saying so. It is deliberately closed rather than put to use: by the time it turns up, the attempt that asked for it has already been reported as failed and the next one may have opened a connection of its own, possibly to a different address — so keeping the straggler would leave two live connections with whichever finished last quietly deciding which one the sponsor sends transactions through.
- **A rebuilt connection came back blind, and would have been rebuilt for ever** (stagenet sponsor, found by a live drill on 2026/09/07). The sponsor's node was deliberately black-holed for nine minutes. Spotting it worked and the connection was rebuilt as it should have been — but what came back was submitting transactions perfectly well while telling the sponsor nothing about the chain, and nothing was ever going to fix that. Three faults, all of them now closed. The first: when a rebuild failed, the sponsor kept the dead connection's last reading instead of forgetting it, so it went on comparing a position from ten minutes ago against a chain that was still moving — which reads as a connection falling further behind every minute, and would have had it rebuilding a perfectly good connection every five minutes for as long as the outage lasted. A connection that has been torn down now reports that it knows nothing, because it does. The second: the subscription that tells the sponsor about new blocks was only ever attached in one place, so a connection that recovered any other way, or one whose subscription failed to attach, stayed silent for the rest of the day. It is now attached to every connection the sponsor opens — the first one, a rebuilt one, and one that recovers on its own — after waiting for the client to be ready, since asking a moment too early is itself a way to end up blind. Each one writes a line saying so, and a fresh connection starts as "nothing known yet" rather than inheriting the old one's position, so it gets its own five minutes before anything is concluded about it. The third: there was no repair for a connection that was working but silent. The sponsor now tells apart a client that cannot offer the subscription at all — nothing to be done, and nothing is attempted — from one that can and whose attempt failed, which is one call away from working. The second earns exactly that call, and deliberately not a rebuild: the connection is fine, and rebuilding it would throw away a working one every five minutes. Also fixed: the status page was showing the connection's state as of the last health check rather than as it is now, so during the drill it showed the remains of a connection that had been repaired forty-seven seconds earlier.
- **The sponsor called itself broken 614 times in a day while it was perfectly well, and stayed quiet through a real outage** (stagenet sponsor, 2026/09/06). The check added the day before to spot a lost connection watched the wrong thing. It compared how far the chain had got against the sponsor's own internal counters, and concluded that a chain producing blocks while those counters stood still meant the sponsor had been cut off. Those counters only move when something happens on chain that concerns this particular wallet, so on a quiet afternoon they stand still for hours with nothing at all wrong — and the check fired on every single look, 614 times in a day, each one writing an alarming line into the journal and re-reading a wallet that was fine. Worse, the same day showed the check could be blind exactly when it was needed: it asked one public node how far the chain had got, and when that node's addresses stopped answering it went blind at the same moment the connection did, so the sponsor's status page said connected and healthy for seven and a half minutes while nothing it sent could get through. Both are fixed by watching the connection itself. The sponsor now keeps a live subscription to new blocks on the very connection it submits transactions over, so a block arriving there is proof that connection is alive — and unlike the internal counters, blocks arrive whether or not anything in them concerns this wallet, so silence means something. It is compared against how far the chain has got according to two independent sources, the public node and the indexer, which are different machines answering different protocols and so cannot go quiet together for one reason; whichever is further along is used, and losing both is treated as knowing nothing rather than as bad news. Five minutes of the connection sitting forty blocks behind, or five minutes of silence on it while forty blocks were produced, rebuilds the connection — on the first look, with no waiting. A connection whose client cannot offer the subscription at all is treated as unknown rather than as dead. The old half-hour rule on the internal counters is kept exactly as it was, because it is looking at something else. Added alongside: if the indexer itself falls a hundred blocks behind for five minutes, the sponsor says so and does nothing — everything it knows about the chain comes from there, so an operator reading stale figures should know why, but there is no repair here for somebody else's server, and this notice can never restart anything or hurry any other fault along. The status page now carries the connection's own view of the chain and how far behind it is on every check, healthy ones included, so the rule can be watched not firing as well as firing.
- **Nothing said the sponsor was running out of money until it had** (stagenet sponsor, 2026/09/06). The droplet supervisor watches for things a restart repairs — a dead connection, a wedged balance, a proxy answering 502 — and the one fault it had nothing to say about is the one restarting cannot touch: the wallet that pays for everybody's names and opening balances slowly emptying. Nobody would find out until a registration failed for want of funds, at which point the fix is a faucet and a person, and both are minutes away at best. The supervisor now reads the two balances it already fetches every minute and says so while there is still time to act: below 500 NIGHT, which is roughly a week of grants at the current ceilings, and below a fifth of a healthy DUST balance. Both are alert-only — nothing is restarted, no strike is taken, and the droplet is not called unhealthy for being low on stock, because it is answering every request perfectly while it is. Each says so at most once every six hours, since a balance that is low stays low and a reminder repeated every minute is a reminder nobody reads. Either can be turned off, and a build that does not publish a balance is never mistaken for a sponsor with none. Fixed alongside it: the supervisor and the service itself held different figures for how often the same DUST repair may be attempted — ten minutes inside the process, five outside it — so the shorter one quietly won. Both are ten minutes now.
- **The sponsor had one node and one indexer, and no way to give it a second of either** (stagenet sponsor, 2026/09/06). Everything the sponsor does passes through two addresses that are not ours and carry no promise of being up: the Midnight node it submits transactions to, and the indexer it reads the chain from. Both were single addresses baked into the service, so a bad afternoon at either one was a sponsor that could not register a name, could not pay in an opening balance, and could not balance a fee — with nothing an operator could do about it but wait for somebody else to fix their server. Both settings now take a list of addresses written in the order they should be tried, and a single address on its own behaves exactly as it always did: it is asked, and its answer or its failure is the whole outcome, with no extra request and no new code path. Where there is a list, it is tried left to right; nothing reorders it, nothing balances load across it, and nothing remembers which one answered last time, so the preferred address is asked first every time and an operator can prove the fallback works by writing the list the other way round. The connection the sponsor submits transactions on walks the list every time it is rebuilt — starting again at the first address, so a provider that has come back is picked up without restarting the service — and the check that asks the node how far the chain has got walks it on every reading. Indexer questions fall through to the next address only when an indexer could not be asked at all: an indexer that answers and says it has not seen a transaction has settled the question, and a second, further-behind opinion is not allowed to overturn it. One limit is stated rather than papered over: the wallet library builds its own two connections once, at start-up, and offers no way to re-point them, so those stay where they opened until the service restarts — what the list buys them is the choice of which address to open on, made by asking each in turn before the wallet is built. `/status` now names the addresses that were configured and the ones actually in use, so a fall-through is visible rather than silent, and a fall-through that succeeds writes a line saying which address picked the sponsor up.
- **One bad host could still take the demo down, twice over** (2026/09/06). Two of the addresses the app is built with had no second name to fall back on. The indexer is the service every Passport reads the chain through, and when it was unreachable a Passport would not open at all; when it went quiet mid-session the balances simply stopped moving, with nothing on screen to say why. The Passport service — the one that registers a `.night` name and pays in the opening balance — was a single address too, so an afternoon of its downtime was an afternoon of queued names. Both variables now take a list of addresses, written in the order they should be tried, and one address on its own behaves exactly as it always did. A Passport opens on the first indexer that answers a quick health question, and an indexer that drops its connection or stops making progress for ninety seconds is replaced: the wallet is rebuilt on the next address in the list, from the records already on the device and resuming from where it had got to, which the reader sees as a reconnection rather than a fresh start. The list is rotated rather than shortened, so a repaired first choice is picked up again without a rebuild of the app. The Passport service is different, and deliberately stricter: a standby is a separate wallet with its own money and its own record of who it has already paid, so it is only ever asked when the first service DID NOT ANSWER — nothing at the far end, a request that timed out, or the page a proxy sends when the service behind it is down. Anything the service says for itself, including every refusal and every "too many just now", ends there and is shown to the reader exactly as one service's refusal is shown today; asking a second wallet behind a refusal would register the same name twice or pay the opening balance twice. One case remains that a browser cannot rule out: the first service acted and its answer was lost on the way back, in which case the standby may act again — that is bounded by the checks already in place, which read what has already been paid before every attempt, so a Passport can end up with more than it was due but never with less and never without its name. Nothing a reader sees has changed; a failover writes one line to the developer console naming which address in the list was used.
- **Nothing was watching the sponsor as a whole, so it stayed broken for five hours** (stagenet sponsor, 2026/09/05). The droplet that pays everybody’s fees had a watchdog, and the watchdog only ever asked one question: is the balancer answering? It was. It answered every check for four and a half hours while every registration, every activation grant, and every mUSD mint failed, because the thing that had died — the connection it submits transactions on — was not one of the things anybody was looking at. Nothing restarted it, and nothing told anybody. The watchdog is now a supervisor: every minute it walks the whole path a person actually walks — the public address, the proxy in front, the sponsor itself, and the proof server beside it — and it reads the sponsor’s own health page properly rather than looking for one word in it, so a sponsor that cannot register a name, cannot fund an account, cannot reach the chain, or has fallen behind it is unhealthy even while it is cheerfully answering. Three bad minutes in a row and it restarts the part that is at fault, and only that part: the sponsor, the proxy, or the prover. It will not interrupt a payment that is in flight — somebody is watching that screen — unless the job has plainly been stuck for ten minutes. It waits ten minutes before a second restart of the same thing, then twenty, then forty, so a dependency that is genuinely down cannot be turned into a service that restarts every minute. And after three restarts in two hours that have not helped it stops, writes down why, and says so every minute until a person looks — because at that point restarting is not the repair, it is only hiding the fault. An optional webhook carries every one of those decisions to wherever the team is looking, with a daily heartbeat so a dead webhook is noticed on a quiet day rather than during the next outage; with none set, nothing is sent. No change to what the sponsor spends, or to any cap or budget.
- **A name the service refused made the reader press a button** (2026/09/05). When the service that registers names failed at its own work — on 2026/09/05 it could not deploy the piece that makes `<name>.night` resolve, so nothing was registered — Passport stopped on "The claim did not complete" and waited for a human. Nothing in that refusal was a fact about the reader or about the name they had chosen, and the same request a minute later usually works; the opening balance, refused by the same service on the same afternoon, quietly asked again for ten minutes without troubling anybody. The claim is now just as patient. It keeps the progress view it was showing, says "The name service is busy. Trying again…" with a live countdown to the next attempt, and asks again after 10 s, 20 s, 40 s, 80 s and then every two minutes, for up to eight minutes in all — or sooner, where the service says when it will be free. Try again is still there: during the wait it reads "Try now" and asks straight away, and "Continue to Home" is still beside it. A name that lands on a later attempt ends exactly as one that lands on the first — same confirmation, same entry in the activity list — and the opening balance is held back until it does, as it already was. Refusals that time cannot fix are not retried and show the same card they always did: a name already taken, a name the service would not accept, and too many names registered lately. Nor is anything retried that could mean the name has already been registered, which would be the one way to pay for it twice.
- **The sponsor waited half an hour before admitting it had lost the chain** (stagenet sponsor, 2026/09/05). The connection the sponsor reads the chain through died at about 14:48 UTC and the first word of it came at 15:28 — "the wallet's sync indices have not moved in 40 min". That delay was built in rather than accidental. The only staleness check the sponsor had watched its own counters and waited thirty minutes for them to stand still, because from the inside a chain with nothing happening on it and a wallet that has stopped being told what is happening look exactly the same: counters that do not move. Thirty minutes was how long a quiet stagenet was judged able to go without producing anything the sponsor cares about, so the fault had to be tolerated for just as long. The sponsor now asks somebody else. On every health check it makes one small request to the public Midnight node — over its own separate connection, so nothing that has died can answer on its behalf — and reads the height of the newest block. Stagenet produces a block about every six seconds whatever the sponsor is doing, so forty blocks produced while the sponsor learned nothing at all is no longer explainable as a quiet chain. That is now reported after about five minutes instead of forty, and the repair it earns rebuilds the submission connection as well as re-reading the wallet. The sponsor also looks at itself every two minutes rather than every ten, because a five-minute rule checked every ten minutes is a ten-minute rule; a check costs one reading of figures the sponsor already holds in memory plus the one small request above, so there was nothing to save by looking less often. The checks that can restart the service were re-timed to match, so the faster cadence has not made the sponsor any more willing to bounce itself: it still takes half an hour of a fault that will not clear before a restart is asked for, and the one repair that ends the process now has a stated ten-minute floor of its own instead of inheriting one from how often the sponsor happened to look. It still stands off entirely while a wallet is being spent from. The old half-hour rule is kept exactly as it was and is what runs whenever the public node cannot be reached, so losing the second opinion costs the speed and nothing else — nothing is ever concluded from the public node failing to answer. The request is bounded at five seconds, cannot interrupt anything, and never on its own leads to a restart. `/status` now carries the public head, how far it has climbed while this wallet stood still, and, after ten failures in a row, the fact that the second opinion is unavailable.
- **Tokens were shown under stand-in glyphs rather than their own marks** (2026/09/05). NIGHT was drawn as a crescent moon and the two dollar stablecoins as a generic stack of coins — icons picked from the icon set because they were to hand, not because they said anything about the asset. A reader who knows what these things look like still had to read the ticker to find out which one they were looking at, which is the whole job the mark beside it was there to do. The three colours Passport can name now carry their real artwork: the Midnight brand pack's own symbol, geometry unchanged, and the USD-coin mark, drawn as a clean vector rather than an embedded picture so it stays sharp at 20 px. The Midnight symbol ships in both of the pack's colour ways and the one that reads against the surface is chosen from the theme, including when the theme is following the operating system and the operating system changes under it. The marks appear wherever an amount already carried one — the balance rows on Home, the token shelf on Assets, and the Send sheet's amount field and review step — and Passport Swap's own hand-drawn green dollar has been replaced by the same mark. `describeColour` remains the single authority on which colour gets which, so no two screens can show the same holding differently, and a colour nothing can name keeps the plain glyph it always had rather than borrowing another asset's artwork.
- **Every claim, grant, and mUSD mint failed for four and a half hours** (stagenet sponsor, 2026/09/05). Anyone who set up a Passport between 15:30 and 20:12 UTC saw "The claim did not complete. <name>.night was not registered, and your name is kept for you." — every time, however often they pressed it. Activation grants and mUSD mints failed the same way. The cause was one websocket. The sponsor talks to the Midnight node over a connection it opens once and keeps, and at 14:48 that connection died; the public node was healthy the whole time and the library underneath was meant to reconnect on its own, but it had got stuck deciding it was already reconnecting and never came back. Five hundred and twenty submissions were refused instantly by a socket that was not there, while every health signal the sponsor publishes — synced, not busy, sponsorships available — went on saying it was well, because those describe a different connection: the one it reads the chain from, which was fine. The one check that did fire, forty minutes in, re-read the wallet and reported itself fixed in 0 s without so much as looking at the socket. A restart cleared it at once. The sponsor now builds a brand-new connection the moment one is shown to be dead — rather than waiting on a reconnect it cannot see — and retries the failed submission on it; it counts consecutive failures, publishes them, rebuilds the socket as part of every health check that finds it down, and restarts itself if a fresh connection cannot be made either. The droplet watchdog restarts the service independently if it sees three of these failures in two minutes with nothing getting through. Fixed alongside it: through the whole outage the sponsor kept pre-minting a spare mUSD coin on a fixed one-minute cadence, spending about 7.4 seconds of proving on each doomed attempt — 249 of them in five hours. That retry now backs off from 15 seconds to 8 minutes after each failure and stops entirely while nothing can be submitted. No change to what the sponsor spends, or to any cap or budget.
- **Firefox froze at "Encrypting your Passport state on this device"** (found by the cross-browser suite, 2026/09/05). After writing the new Passport, onboarding asked the browser to keep its storage persistent and waited for the answer. Firefox asks the reader that question with a small permission pop-up, and until it is pressed the browser simply does not answer — so anyone who ignored or missed it, and every automated Firefox run, stayed on that sentence with no error and no way on. The request now waits three seconds and then moves on; an answer given later still takes effect. Nothing about the passkey, the account, or the state just written depends on it.
- **Two sentences that were untrue on Safari** (found by the Safari review, 2026/09/05). The install sheet told everybody the home-screen app "keeps you signed in". On an iPhone it does not: an installed web app gets its own storage, so the passkey follows through iCloud Keychain but the local records stay behind in Safari and the app asks for the passkey on first open. iPhone readers now see "It opens full-screen and is one tap away next time. You will sign in once more with your passkey."; where the browser offers the install itself the app does inherit the session, and that sheet is unchanged. Separately, the passkey-timeout message advised trying a private window, which on Safari has no usable storage — following it lands somebody in the one place a Passport cannot be saved, at the moment they are already stuck. The advice is gone; the extension hint, which is a real and observed cause, stays.
- **Tapping any Send field zoomed the page in on an iPhone and never zoomed back out** (found by the Safari review, 2026/09/05). The asset, recipient, and amount fields were 15px and the address box 12.5px; iOS Safari zooms the viewport on focus below 16px and does not undo it on blur, so a reader was left pinching their way out of the page mid-payment. All four are 16px now, with the rule written beside the number. Fixed alongside it: the app's root wrapper measured itself against the viewport with the address bar collapsed while every screen inside it measured the visible one, so screens scrolled about 60-90px when they looked like they fitted and the bottom bar seemed to drift as the address bar came and went. Root and screens now agree.
- **A partner app connecting from an installed iPhone Passport waited three minutes and heard nothing** (found by the Safari review, 2026/09/05). Opening a window from a home-screen web app lands it in Safari with no link back to the app that opened it, and both consent sheets read that as "no request here" — so Passport showed an ordinary sign-in page over a request nobody could see, and the app, which watches for the window closing, could not tell either. The sheets now tell the two facts apart. Where the app also sent a signed return link, the answer goes back that way and the redirect sheet is the only one shown. Where it did not, Passport says so immediately: "Passport has no way to send an answer back to the app that opened this. Return to the app and try again, or open its link in Safari." A payment launch in the same position says the same thing and adds that nothing has been sent.
- **A proof interrupted on an iPhone left a spinner that never resolved, and an over-quota device reported a key it had just downloaded as missing** (found by the Safari review, 2026/09/05). In-browser proving is the default wherever no proof server is configured, which is every stagenet build. The channel to the prover had no timeout and no cancellation, and iOS reclaims memory by jettisoning the prover outright without telling the page — so switching to another app mid-send could hang the send for ever, worst of all on the second leg of a shielded payment, after the first has already moved. The channel now watches for silence — six minutes of it for a proof, which is silent from start to finish while the arithmetic runs, and ninety seconds for the far quicker validation pass — then restarts once and retries, and otherwise says "The proof did not finish on this device. Keep this screen open and try again — leaving Passport while it works can stop it part-way." Every request's duration is now logged, so those two figures can be replaced by measurements from real phones. Proving keys are handed over by transfer and cached on the far side, so a repeat proof re-sends nothing. Separately, the service worker's cache write is now best effort: a phone out of storage was turning a 54 MB download that had arrived perfectly into a network error, surfaced as advice to run a staging script.
- **Safari raised a second passkey prompt nobody had pressed anything for** (found by the Safari review, 2026/09/05). Safari does not evaluate the key material during passkey creation, so a Passport made there needs one more confirmation — and onboarding fired it automatically, a line after the creation resolved, with the press that authorised the creation already spent. Safari is the strictest browser there is about that: at best a prompt arrived on its own, at worst it was refused and onboarding ended holding a passkey it could not open a Passport with. It is now a question on the screen — "Your passkey is ready. This device needs to see it once more to finish setting up your Passport." — with "Confirm with your passkey" beneath it, and the press is what raises the prompt. Platforms that do evaluate at creation see none of this and still onboard on one prompt.
- **Dismissing the passkey sheet raised a make-a-passkey sheet nobody asked for** (found by the Safari review, 2026/09/05). A cancelled picker looked to the onboarding path exactly like an empty one, and both fell straight through to creating a credential — so on iOS the gesture that meant "not now" was answered a moment later by a second prompt, and on a browser whose site data had been cleared it is how somebody ended up with a second, empty Passport beside the one they were trying to reopen. A dismissal now ends the ceremony: nothing is created, and the screen shows the panel that says the passkey could not be loaded, offering "Create a new passkey" and "Use a different passkey". Those two controls matter — a dismissed sheet and an empty one are the same event to the browser, so the answer has to work for the person whose passkey was deleted from Google Password Manager as well as for the person who simply changed their mind, and telling either of them to press the button that just failed is a loop. A discovery that failed for any other reason still goes on to enrol, guarded as before.
- **A fresh Safari or iOS container could not sign in without asking for an extension it may not have** (found by the Safari review, 2026/09/05). The discoverable assertion — the one ceremony a container with no local record can run — asked for the largeBlob extension unconditionally, and the opt-out added on 2026/09/04 reads a local profile, which an installed iOS Passport does not have by definition. That is the same combination already known to leave Chrome on Android with a passkey sheet that never settles. Discovery now sends no largeBlob slice at all, so nothing about the extension can narrow the picker or hold the sheet open; support is still taken for free where a client volunteers it. A caller that genuinely wants the stored account details may ask for one extra targeted read, bounded and taken down when the bound expires, and every way it can fail comes back as "no blob" with the sign-in already done. Passport's own sign-in does not ask: "I already have a name — find my Passport" reaches the same account from any device.
- **A passkey with no PRF extension was refused with a false diagnosis and no way out** (found by the Android-shape suite, 2026/09/04). On an Android or Google Password Manager combination that implements neither largeBlob nor PRF, enrolment made the passkey, found it could derive no key, and the landing screen said "Passport passkeys require a valid HTTPS origin or localhost relying-party domain" — on localhost, about an origin that was fine, as a bare banner with nothing under it and both remaining buttons leading back to the same failure. The sentence was an accident: the refusal ended "…or PRF-capable security key", and the backend's message mapping decided by substring over the text and matched the word "security". The refusal now travels as itself rather than as a string, the mapping decides by the error's name instead of its wording, and the screen says "This passkey cannot be used for Passport on this device — try a different passkey or device." with "Use a different passkey" beneath it — the door that reaches a passkey held on a phone. It deliberately does not offer "Create a new passkey": the passkey this device makes is the thing that failed, so a second one is the same passkey.
- **The back gesture left Passport instead of closing the sheet on top of it** (found by the Android-shape suite, 2026/09/04). Send and Receive were React state and put nothing on the history stack, so on Android — where back is how anything is dismissed — a back swipe over an open Send sheet navigated the document away, taking the sheet, the amount typed into it, and the session's warm wallet with it. Installed to the home screen, with nothing behind Passport to go back to, the same gesture closed the app. Each sheet now owns one history entry: back closes the sheet and nothing else, and a sheet closed by its own control takes its entry back off the stack so the reader's next back press is not swallowed. The URL is untouched, so a launch carrying an app's callback parameters still reloads into the same place.
- **Android: stuck with an orphan passkey; a new passkey kept showing the old failed profile; "The passkey prompt did not finish"** (reported by Hector, 2026/09/04). Three causes: the name record was keyed by network rather than by passkey, so any passkey inherited it; the app asked Android's platform passkeys (Google Password Manager) for the largeBlob extension they do not support, which narrows Chrome's account sheet to nothing and the prompt never settles; and there was no way to start clean. Now: every local record is bound to the passkey that owns it and handed back only to a passkey that can be shown to own the account; no largeBlob request is made to a passkey without it; "I already have a name — find my Passport" restores an account by its .night name, verified on chain against the passkey (one prompt), which is the only door on Android since there is no blob there; "Set up a new Passport on this device" forgets this device's records and starts the create path without touching the passkey or the chain. Also fixed while reproducing: sign-out during the silent restore was undone by it, and a legacy record survived the way out. (319eb78)
- **Releases did not say what they fixed** (Hector, 2026/09/04). The tag step now refuses to run without these notes. (4a2e599, 6932d99)
- **A refused registration still spent a grant coin** (found by the 2026/09/04 sponsor soak). A signup refused its name at the sponsor's hourly ceiling at 17:38:02 was funded a second later anyway, and ended holding NIGHT and mUSD with no name against it — a grant that is once per account, ever, spent on a Passport nobody can send to by name. The opening balance is fired the moment the account lands rather than after the name answers, so the two race and both orders happen; a refusal that leaves the name queued now holds the account's grant, is read before every attempt in the schedule rather than only at the call site, and is lifted where a name registers — which is the queued name's "Register now" as well as onboarding, since both run the one claim.
- **A ceiling refusal told the reader the sponsor was busy and their name would register on its own** (found by the 2026/09/04 sponsor soak). Neither half was true: the sponsor was answering in 60 ms with four spend lanes open and refused in 25 ms without attempting anything, and nothing was ever going to register that name — the only thing that will is somebody pressing Register now. A reader who believed it waited for something that could not happen. A refusal because too many names have been registered lately now says so and says what to do, with the Try again and Continue to Home controls unchanged; the busy sentence is kept for the refusals that genuinely mean a spend of the sponsor's own is in the way.
- **"Your account is ready" beside a balance of mUSD 0** (found by the 2026/09/04 stability audit). The opening balance is two deposits and they do not arrive together, but the "On the way" row retired the moment the account held anything at all — so a new Passport lost the row as the NIGHT landed and then sat for 18.1 seconds showing mUSD 0 with nothing on screen saying the stablecoin was still coming. The row now waits for BOTH halves of the grant. A refusal or an exhausted schedule still ends the wait exactly as before, including when one half did land, and a build whose sponsor names no stablecoin colour is unaffected. (8388fdb)
- **A busy sponsor was passed over instead of waited for** (found by the 2026/09/04 stability audit). The balancer answers `429 PENDING_TRANSACTION` with a `retryAfterMs` when it is already balancing something — a queue position, not a refusal — and the client ignored the figure and posted the same transaction to the 1AM gateway instead, throwing away a sponsor that was two seconds from saying yes and building the leg elsewhere against a contract state the first one was already moving. The hint is now honoured where it was given: up to three waits on the same endpoint, for as long as the sponsor asked inside the existing clamp, drawn from the caller's existing window so the total wait is unchanged. Still busy after that and the fall-through happens as before. Every other retryable refusal is untouched. (2119201)
- **A payment interrupted by a reload sat waiting to be pressed** (found by the 2026/09/04 stability audit).
