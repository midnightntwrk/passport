How Passport works

One account is one contract on the Midnight ledger. No seed phrase exists anywhere: each device holds a secret derived from its passkey, the chain holds only hashes and proofs, and losing every device does not lose the account. This page shows the whole system on one screen, walks the account lifecycle in six steps, and ends with what the October demo puts on a screen.

The lifecycle

Six steps, one account

The life of an account, in order. Each card ends with exactly what crossed the line to the chain, because that boundary is the whole design.

1

Create

Your first device makes a passkey, and the passkey output becomes the device secret. The device deploys your account contract carrying two hashes: a commitment to the device secret, and a commitment to a freshly generated recovery secret.

Crosses commit(device) + commit(recovery) · both secrets stay on the device
2

Add a device

A second device makes its own passkey and secret. Any already-enrolled device authorises the addition with a proof, and the newcomer’s commitment is enrolled at the current epoch. There is no primary device: all devices are equals.

Crosses one proof + one commitment
3

Receive

Anyone may fund the account, no authority required. Night lands in the public balance. A shielded token lands as a sealed commitment: an observer learns that something arrived, but not the amount and not the token type. The belief that contract-held shielded coins must be public was proven wrong on a real node; the private pattern is the standard.

Crosses the deposit itself · nothing about you
4

Spend

Money out always requires authority. The device proves knowledge of a secret whose commitment is enrolled at the current epoch; the circuit checks the proof, moves the funds, and bumps the round counter. An impostor cannot produce that proof, so the ledger itself rejects the call.

Crosses one proof · the secret never moves
5

Delegate

Connecting to a dApp never hands over your keys. The device enrols a separate grant secret for the dApp, scoped on-chain to one token color and a cumulative cap. The circuit enforces the cap, refuses the spend that would exceed it, and revocation makes the dApp’s secret worthless whatever it has stored.

Crosses proofs · the scope is public, the grant secret is not
6

Recover

Guardians make total loss survivable. When asked, each guardian re-derives their share on demand from their own key: nothing was stored, so nothing could be lost or leaked. A replacement device reads the public points from the chain, collects a quorum of shares, rebuilds the recovery secret entirely off-chain, and finishes with one transaction: a fresh device commitment, a fresh recovery commitment, and an epoch bump that retires every old device and grant at once. Same account, same address, same name, same balances: only the authority over them changed.

Crosses one proof + two fresh commitments · the guardian roster never appears
Load-bearing rule. Every published backup uses a fresh secret and a fresh session nonce, and any change to the guardian set is a full re-ceremony. Rotation is what makes removing a guardian real.
Evidence

Why we can claim this works

Nothing above is speculative. Every mechanism runs for real: the reference implementation at contract/ realises the two published standards in one deployment, with both conformance suites passing against a local Midnight network.

Private custody

Contract-held shielded coins do not have to expose amount or token type; the widely copied vault recipe did so as a bookkeeping choice (and silently lost change, a defect found and fixed along the way). The validated private pattern is normative in MIP-0012.

MIP-0012 the experiment
One authorisation seam

Every authorised circuit performs the same check through a single helper circuit, instantiated with in-circuit JubJub Schnorr per MIP-0013. One epoch bump retires every device and grant at once, which is exactly what recovery relies on.

MIP-0013 architecture
Storage-free recovery

Recovery adopts BUSS, the bottom-up secret sharing construction from the ANARKey paper: guardians derive shares on demand and store nothing, and the on-chain footprint is a commitment plus a few public points.

The October demo

The lifecycle above, made watchable: an end-to-end Passport account on a screen, with a recovery sad-path. The demo is an MVP that consumes the standards early. Where it takes a shortcut (single device, managed signing through a partner-operated MPC committee), the shortcut is flagged below and carries a migration plan back to the principled path; the standards body lands that path on a continuous timeline. A step-by-step onboarding click-through of the first-run experience is kept in the archive.

  • Passkey loginFirst visit registers a passkey (Face ID, Touch ID, Windows Hello). Subsequent visits authenticate biometrically. No seed phrase shown at any point.
  • Name registrationUser registers alice.passport.night against the deployed upstream name service (MIP-0007); on-chain confirmation visible. Name uniqueness enforced at the registry.
  • Wallet viewShortcutThree role addresses (Shielded, Night, Dust) with balances synced via view key and a hosted indexer. The v1.0 path is the substitutable multi-provider indexer model.
  • Signing primitiveUser signs arbitrary data and a Night transfer. Signatures verify cross-implementation (TypeScript signer ↔ Rust verifier).
  • Function-call key grantsUser issues, rotates, and revokes scoped function-call keys for dApp authorisation. Multi-device peer behaviour is deferred.
  • Sign-in with PassportA pseudo third-party app requests scoped access; user approves with biometric; transaction signed; function-call key issued.
  • Recovery walkthroughShortcutUser "loses" their passkey. Recovery via the auth provider’s flow: alt-passkey on a fallback device or backup credential. On-chain account state unchanged. The v1.0 path is the guardian recovery of lifecycle step 6, already running in the prototype.
